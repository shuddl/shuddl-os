## Detention is the most provable theft in trucking — and the proof now fits in a driver's pocket

Most of what leaks out of a 10-to-100-truck carrier leaks quietly. Deadhead, a soft lane, a rate you should have walked away from — you find those at month-end, in aggregate, long after you could have done anything about them.

Detention is different. It has a start time and an end time. It happens at a fixed street address. A person you employ is standing there the entire time it occurs, holding a device that knows where he is and what time it is.

Of every dollar that leaves your business in a year, this is the one category where the evidence exists at the exact moment of the loss. And it is still, by a wide margin, the dollar you are least likely to collect.

### Start with the size of it

The American Transportation Research Institute studied detention across the industry in 2023. Drivers were detained on **39.3% of all stops** — not 39.3% of stops at your worst consignee, 39.3% of every stop. Refrigerated freight ran **56.2%**. Carriers working the spot market ran **42.5%**.

Per driver, that came to **117 to 209 hours a year**, depending on sector. Across the industry, ATRI put it at **$3.6 billion in direct expense to motor carriers plus $11.5 billion in lost productivity — $15.1 billion**, covering 135 million hours and 72 million gallons of diesel burned at idle. ([ATRI, 2023](https://truckingresearch.org/2024/09/new-research-documents-substantial-financial-and-safety-impacts-from-truck-driver-detention/))

Industry totals don't pay bills, so put it on your own P&L. ATRI's marginal cost of operating a truck was **$90.89 per hour** in 2024 ([ATRI, 2024](https://truckingresearch.org/2025/07/new-atri-report-shows-trucking-profitability-severly-squeezed-by-high-costs-low-rates/)). Multiply that by the 117–209 hour band and one seat carries roughly **$10,600 to $19,000** of detention exposure a year. Twenty-five trucks: **$266,000 to $475,000**.

Be honest about that arithmetic — it is my multiplication of two ATRI figures, not a published finding, and not every detained hour is fully incremental or fully billable. So cut it in half. It is still larger than most line items you spent last quarter negotiating. For scale: the truckload sector's average operating margin was **−2.3%** in 2024 ([ATRI, 2024](https://truckingresearch.org/2025/07/new-atri-report-shows-trucking-profitability-severly-squeezed-by-high-costs-low-rates/)). Detention isn't a rounding error against your margin. It is bigger than your margin.

### Now the part that should make you angry

The OOIDA Foundation surveyed more than 1,250 drivers in 2023. **18% said they never receive detention pay.** Only **29%** receive it on all loads.

The number that matters most is smaller and worse: **9% of the drivers who always request detention pay are never paid it.** ([OOIDA Foundation, 2023](https://www.ooida.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/2023-Detention-Time-Survey-FINAL.pdf))

Those drivers did everything the process asks. They asked, every time, and lost anyway. That is not a discipline problem and it is not a rate-negotiation problem. It is an evidence problem.

Here is what a detention claim actually is at the moment it gets disputed: one party's recollection against another party's records. Records win. They always win. Your driver remembers sitting at that dock from 06:40 to 11:15. The receiver has a gate log that says something else, or says nothing at all. You have a note in a dispatch screen, typed hours later by someone who wasn't there, and a bill-of-lading scan with a handwritten time on it in your own driver's handwriting. You are not going to win that argument, and after you lose it twice you stop having it. That's how a $266,000-a-year exposure becomes invisible.

Call it what you want — if "theft" is too strong, call it uncompensated use of your truck and your driver. The mechanism is the same either way: the loss is real, the proof was available, and nobody captured it in a form that survives a dispute.

### What a claim needs to survive

Six things, and the sixth is the one everybody skips:

1. Arrival time — recorded when it happened, not reconstructed.
2. Departure time — same.
3. Where, to the foot.
4. Who was there.
5. What the condition was — photographs, not adjectives.
6. **A record that neither side can quietly edit after the argument starts.**

Nothing about that list is technically hard in 2026. The driver is already carrying a device that knows the time and the place. The gap is that the capture in most cabs was built for someone else's question. An electronic logging record proves duty status to a federal auditor; it was never designed to prove that this truck sat at that door for four hours and thirty-five minutes.

Meanwhile the office that would chase the claim keeps getting thinner. More than a third of trucking employees are already non-drivers, and the ratio has moved the wrong way: **8.2 drivers per office manager, down from 11.3 across 2009–19** ([BLS, 2021](https://www.freightwaves.com/news/by-the-numbers-driver-to-non-driver-ratio)). Nobody is sitting idle waiting to assemble a detention file.

This is what we've been building at SHUDDL, and it is a narrow claim: arrival and departure are captured as two separately signed records by the person standing there, each carrying its own time, position, photographs and signature; corrections are new records rather than overwrites, so the chain can be handed to a counterparty intact; it works with no cell signal and syncs when the truck finds one; and the resulting evidence attaches to the invoice at the moment the stop closes, rather than waiting on someone to type it up.
